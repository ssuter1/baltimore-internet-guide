# Baltimore City Residential Internet Guide

A community-maintained, plain-English guide to residential internet providers and broadband technologies available in Baltimore City.

> Internet service availability in Baltimore City can vary significantly by neighborhood, street, building, and even individual address. Always check your exact address with the provider before making a decision. Speeds, plans, and service areas can change as providers upgrade and expand their networks.

## Verizon Fios

Technology: Fiber-to-the-Home (FTTH)  
Connection: Fiber optic  
Speeds: Typically 300/300 Mbps, 500/500 Mbps, or approximately gigabit service (up to 940 Mbps download / 880 Mbps upload). Verizon also offers 2 Gig and 5 Gig Fios service in some areas.

Verizon Fios is a fiber-optic internet service. Unlike cable internet, which typically uses coaxial cable for the final connection to your home, or DSL, which uses traditional copper telephone wiring, Fios runs fiber-optic cable directly to the property.

Fiber carries data using light rather than electrical signals over copper wiring. It can provide very high speeds in both directions, which is why Fios commonly offers similar or symmetrical download and upload speeds. This can be especially useful for working from home, uploading large files, cloud backups, video conferencing, and other activities that send a significant amount of data back to the internet.

At the home, Verizon typically installs an Optical Network Terminal (ONT). The ONT converts the optical signal traveling over the fiber into a connection that can be used by your home network and router.

### Availability in Baltimore City

Fios availability within Baltimore City has historically been much more limited than in many of the surrounding Baltimore suburbs. Availability can vary considerably from one neighborhood, street, or even address to another, so residents should check their specific address with Verizon rather than assume Fios is available throughout their neighborhood.

There are signs that Verizon is again expanding its fiber network within the city. As of 2026, residents have reported new Fios construction and availability in areas including Remington and Fells Point. Because this deployment is ongoing and highly address-specific, the best way to determine availability is to check your exact address with Verizon.

## Verizon DSL

Technology: DSL (Digital Subscriber Line)  
Connection: Traditional copper telephone lines  
Status: Legacy service being phased out

Verizon DSL is one of the older forms of broadband internet still found in parts of Baltimore. DSL uses the same copper telephone wiring originally installed for traditional landline telephone service to carry an internet connection.

Unlike old dial-up internet, DSL allows the telephone line to carry voice and internet data at the same time. A DSL modem at the home communicates over the copper telephone pair with Verizon equipment elsewhere in the neighborhood or at a telephone central office.

One major limitation of DSL is distance. The farther your home is from Verizon's equipment, the weaker the DSL signal becomes and the lower the speeds that can be provided. The age and condition of the copper wiring can also affect performance.

### Speed

DSL is considerably slower than modern fiber and cable internet. Depending on the particular type of DSL, distance from Verizon equipment, and condition of the telephone line, service may range from only a few Mbps to tens of Mbps.

This is very different from Verizon Fios. Although both services are provided by Verizon, Fios uses fiber-optic cable while DSL uses the old copper telephone network.

### Being Phased Out

DSL and the copper telephone network that carries it are now considered legacy technologies. Verizon is in the process of retiring portions of its decades-old copper network and replacing copper-based services with newer technologies such as fiber and fixed wireless.

As of 2026, Verizon has filed applications to retire legacy copper infrastructure in Maryland and several other states. Some proposed transitions may begin on or after November 30, 2026. This does not mean that every remaining Verizon DSL connection in Baltimore will stop working on that date. Copper retirement is occurring in stages and varies by service area. Existing customers should pay attention to notices from Verizon regarding changes at their particular address.

For someone ordering new internet service today, DSL should generally be viewed as a legacy option rather than a modern alternative to cable or fiber.

## Xfinity

Technology: DOCSIS 3.1 cable internet  
Connection: Coaxial cable / Hybrid Fiber-Coaxial (HFC)  
Speeds: Currently up to approximately 2.1 Gbps download / 300 Mbps upload at eligible Baltimore addresses

Xfinity is one of the most widely available wired internet providers in Baltimore City. Its residential internet service is primarily delivered using DOCSIS, a technology that allows high-speed internet service to operate over the same type of coaxial cable traditionally used for cable television.

Xfinity's network is actually a combination of fiber and coaxial cable. Fiber is used throughout much of Comcast's network, but for most Baltimore homes the final connection from the neighborhood network to the house is still coaxial cable. This type of network is commonly called a hybrid fiber-coaxial, or HFC, network.

The current generation of DOCSIS widely used by Xfinity in Baltimore is DOCSIS 3.1. Modern DOCSIS 3.1 networks can provide multi-gigabit download speeds over existing coaxial cable. As of 2026, Xfinity offers Baltimore plans ranging from several hundred Mbps to Gigabit x2, which can provide up to approximately 2.1 Gbps download and 300 Mbps upload at eligible addresses.

Unlike most fiber-to-the-home services, current Xfinity cable service generally provides much faster download speeds than upload speeds. For example, a connection capable of more than 2 Gbps downstream may have an upstream speed of only 300 Mbps. Upload speeds vary depending on the plan, equipment, and network upgrades available at a particular address.

### DOCSIS 4.0

Comcast has begun deploying the next generation of cable technology, DOCSIS 4.0, in select markets. DOCSIS 4.0 greatly increases upstream capacity and allows Comcast to offer symmetrical multi-gigabit service, meaning upload speeds can be as fast as download speeds, while continuing to use coaxial cable for the connection into the home.

Comcast markets its DOCSIS 4.0 symmetrical service as X-Class. As of 2026, DOCSIS 4.0 is still being rolled out and should not be assumed to be available throughout Baltimore City.

## Greenlight Networks

Technology: Fiber-to-the-Home (FTTH)  
Connection: Fiber optic  
Speeds: 500 Mbps, 1 Gbps, 2 Gbps, and 8 Gbps, with symmetrical upload and download speeds

Greenlight Networks is a newer internet provider in Baltimore City that is building its own fiber-to-the-home network. Like Verizon Fios, Greenlight runs fiber-optic cable directly to the property rather than using coaxial cable or traditional copper telephone lines for the final connection.

Greenlight offers symmetrical service, meaning upload and download speeds are the same. Current residential plans range from 500 Mbps to 8 Gbps.

### Availability in Baltimore City

Greenlight began construction in Baltimore in 2025 as part of a planned $100 million investment in its Baltimore-area fiber network. The network is still being built, so availability is currently limited to certain neighborhoods and individual addresses.

As of 2026, reported rollout status includes:

- Mount Washington — construction completed
- Roland Park — active construction
- Hampden — active construction
- Glen — active construction
- Belair-Edison — planned expansion
- Frankford — planned expansion
- Hamilton Hills — planned expansion

Greenlight says additional Baltimore neighborhoods will be added as the network expands. Because the rollout is ongoing, residents should check their exact address rather than assume that service is available throughout one of the listed neighborhoods.

## Port Networks

Technology: Fixed wireless (WiMax/LTU), with fiber or Ethernet service in some apartment and condominium buildings  
Connection: Outdoor wireless radio for most single-family homes; fiber or Ethernet in some multi-unit buildings

Port Networks is a locally based Baltimore internet provider that has served the area for many years. Unlike Xfinity or Verizon, Port does not rely on the traditional cable or telephone network to reach most single-family homes. Instead, it primarily uses fixed wireless technology.

For a typical single-family home, Port installs a small outdoor radio, usually on the roof or another elevated location. This radio communicates wirelessly with one of Port's access points elsewhere in the city. An Ethernet connection then runs from the outdoor equipment into the home and connects to the customer's router. Port identifies WiMax and LTU as technologies used on its wireless network.

### Line of Sight and Availability

Because the connection between the home and Port's network is wireless, availability depends heavily on the location of the property and whether Port can establish a suitable wireless path to one of its access points.

Buildings, trees, terrain, and other obstructions can prevent Port from serving an address even when nearby properties have service. For this reason, Port may need to evaluate the property before determining whether service can be installed. Baltimore residents have also reported being unable to obtain Port service because an adequate line of sight could not be established.

Some users have reported that wireless performance can be affected during particularly bad weather. Port states that its network is designed to provide consistent performance in all weather. As with any fixed wireless service, the quality of the particular wireless link to the property is important.

### Apartments and Condominiums

Port does not use fixed wireless for every customer. In many apartment and condominium buildings, Port delivers service to the building using fiber and connects individual customers through fiber or existing Ethernet cabling. As a result, the technology and speeds available can vary significantly depending on the property.

Port can be an interesting alternative for Baltimore residents who have limited wired broadband choices, particularly at addresses where Xfinity may otherwise be the only high-speed wired provider.

## T-Mobile Home Internet

Technology: 5G Fixed Wireless Access (FWA)  
Connection: T-Mobile 5G/LTE cellular network  
Typical speeds: Approximately 133–498 Mbps download and 12–55 Mbps upload, depending on plan and network conditions

T-Mobile Home Internet is a fixed wireless internet service that uses T-Mobile's cellular network instead of a physical cable or fiber connection to the home. The same general 5G network used by T-Mobile phones provides the internet connection, although Home Internet uses a dedicated gateway designed to remain in the home.

The gateway combines the functions of a cellular modem and Wi-Fi router. It receives the 5G signal from a nearby T-Mobile cell site and then provides Wi-Fi and Ethernet connectivity to devices inside the home. Installation is generally self-service and does not require a technician to run new wiring into the property.

### Speed and Performance

Unlike a wired connection, the performance of T-Mobile Home Internet can vary considerably depending on the location of the home, signal strength, distance from the cell site, network congestion, and even where the gateway is placed inside the house.

T-Mobile currently reports typical download speeds ranging from approximately 133 to 498 Mbps and upload speeds of approximately 12 to 55 Mbps, depending on the plan. Actual speeds can fall above or below these ranges.

Gateway placement can make a significant difference. Placing the gateway near a window or in another location with a strong cellular signal may provide better performance than placing it in a basement or an interior room.

### Availability

T-Mobile Home Internet availability is address-specific. Having good T-Mobile 5G coverage on a phone does not necessarily mean that Home Internet will be offered at the same address.

T-Mobile limits availability based partly on the amount of network capacity available in an area. Because Home Internet customers can use substantially more data than typical phone users, T-Mobile may restrict new Home Internet customers in areas where the cellular network is already heavily utilized.

Because it does not require cable, telephone, or fiber infrastructure to be installed at the property, T-Mobile Home Internet can provide an alternative at Baltimore addresses with limited wired internet choices. However, performance can be more variable than a wired fiber or cable connection because the service depends on cellular signal quality and available network capacity.

## Verizon 5G Home Internet

Technology: 5G Fixed Wireless Access (FWA)  
Connection: Verizon 5G Ultra Wideband cellular network  
Speeds: Up to 1 Gbps download in areas served by Verizon's high-band/mmWave network; speeds vary considerably by location and type of 5G coverage

Verizon 5G Home Internet is a fixed wireless service that provides home internet using Verizon's cellular network rather than a cable or fiber connection to the property.

Verizon's 5G Ultra Wideband network uses two different types of spectrum: mid-band C-band and high-band millimeter wave, commonly called mmWave. Both can be used for 5G Home Internet, but they have very different characteristics.

C-band provides a larger coverage area and better building penetration, while mmWave can provide much higher speeds over considerably shorter distances. Verizon offers 5G Home service capable of up to 1 Gbps download in locations where its high-band/mmWave network is available. Upload speeds are considerably lower and vary depending on the connection.

### Baltimore's Street-Level 5G Network

Baltimore has numerous Verizon 5G small-cell antennas installed at street level, often mounted on utility poles or other structures throughout the city. These smaller antennas allow Verizon to provide high-bandwidth 5G coverage in dense urban neighborhoods without relying entirely on traditional large cellular towers.

This type of deployment is particularly important for mmWave because the signal does not travel nearly as far as lower-frequency cellular signals and is more easily blocked by buildings, trees, and other obstacles.

At some addresses, Verizon's 5G Home equipment may require a suitable path or even direct line of sight to a nearby cell site. Because of this, availability can vary from one house to another even on the same block.

### Equipment and Installation

Depending on the type of 5G service available at an address, Verizon may provide an indoor gateway or a separate 5G receiver. For mmWave service, the receiver may need to be mounted near a window, on an exterior wall, or on a balcony where it can receive a strong signal from the nearby 5G network.

Like T-Mobile Home Internet, Verizon 5G Home does not require traditional cable, telephone, or fiber wiring to reach the house. This can make it another broadband option for Baltimore residents at addresses where wired competition is limited.

Performance and availability are highly address-specific, so residents should check their exact address with Verizon rather than relying solely on the cellular coverage shown for their neighborhood.

## Project Waves

Technology: Fixed wireless and fiber  
Connection: Wireless radio and fiber optic  
Type: Baltimore-based nonprofit community internet provider

Project Waves is a Baltimore-based nonprofit internet service provider focused on providing affordable broadband access to communities that have historically had limited or unaffordable internet options.

Unlike a traditional commercial ISP, Project Waves was created specifically to address Baltimore's digital divide. The organization began building its network using fixed wireless technology, with rooftop equipment throughout the city providing point-to-multipoint wireless connections to participating homes and buildings.

With point-to-multipoint wireless, a central antenna can communicate with equipment installed at multiple nearby properties. This allows an ISP to reach homes without having to run a new physical cable to every property, although buildings, distance, and other obstructions can affect where wireless service can be provided.

Project Waves has also expanded its use of fiber-optic infrastructure. Depending on the location and project, service may therefore be delivered using either wireless technology or fiber rather than a single network technology throughout the city.

### Availability

Project Waves is not a citywide commercial ISP where anyone in Baltimore can necessarily order service. Its network is focused on particular communities, affordable housing developments, and households participating in its broadband programs.

Eligibility and availability therefore depend on the resident's location and the particular programs Project Waves is operating.

Because Project Waves is a nonprofit with a specific digital-equity mission, it should be considered separately from commercial providers such as Xfinity, Verizon, Greenlight, and T-Mobile.

## Starlink

Technology: Low-Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite internet  
Connection: Satellite radio  
Speeds: Variable depending on network conditions, location, and service plan

Starlink is a satellite internet service operated by SpaceX. Unlike every other provider discussed in this guide, Starlink does not require a nearby cable, fiber line, telephone line, or cellular tower. Instead, a small satellite dish installed at the customer's property communicates directly with satellites orbiting above the Earth.

Starlink is significantly different from older satellite internet services. Traditional satellite providers generally use a small number of satellites in geostationary orbit approximately 22,000 miles above the Earth. Because radio signals must travel such a long distance to the satellite and back, these services have traditionally suffered from very high latency.

Starlink instead operates thousands of satellites in low-Earth orbit, only a few hundred miles above the Earth. The much shorter distance substantially reduces latency and allows Starlink to behave much more like a conventional broadband connection.

### Equipment and Line of Sight

Starlink requires a small outdoor dish with a clear view of the sky. The dish electronically tracks Starlink satellites as they pass overhead and automatically switches between satellites as necessary.

Trees, buildings, and other obstructions can interrupt the connection, so dish placement is particularly important. This can present a challenge in dense parts of Baltimore where neighboring rowhouses, trees, and taller buildings may obstruct portions of the sky.

### Use in Baltimore

Starlink is available in the Baltimore area, but it is somewhat different from the other options in this guide. Its biggest advantage is the ability to provide broadband in locations where conventional wired and cellular infrastructure is unavailable.

That advantage is less significant in a densely populated city like Baltimore, where Xfinity and other broadband services are widely available. Starlink also requires specialized equipment and an appropriate location for the satellite dish.

For most Baltimore residents with access to a good cable or fiber connection, Starlink is unlikely to be necessary. However, it provides another option for unusual locations, properties with limited wired service, temporary installations, or residents who specifically want an internet connection that does not depend on the local cable, telephone, or fiber network.

## Useful FCC Resources

### Check What Internet Service Is Available at Your Address

The FCC maintains the National Broadband Map, which allows you to enter your exact address and see which internet providers report that they can provide service to your location. The map shows providers, connection technologies, and reported maximum advertised download and upload speeds.

[FCC National Broadband Map](https://broadbandmap.fcc.gov/)

If the map says a provider serves your address but the provider tells you that service is not actually available, you can submit an Availability Challenge through the map. You can also report an incorrect or missing address.

### Filing a Complaint About an Internet Provider

The FCC also accepts consumer complaints involving internet providers, including issues involving service, speeds, billing, equipment, privacy, and other internet service problems. This can be useful when you have been unable to resolve an issue directly with your provider.

[FCC Consumer Complaint Center](https://consumercomplaints.fcc.gov/)

If your issue is specifically that the FCC Broadband Map incorrectly shows service as available at your address, use the Availability Challenge option on the Broadband Map rather than a general consumer complaint.
